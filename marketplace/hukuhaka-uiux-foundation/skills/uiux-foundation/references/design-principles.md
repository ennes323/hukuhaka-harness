# Design principles and direction

Use this reference when the task needs a design rationale, a new direction, or a judgment about whether an existing direction fits. A principle should help choose between plausible solutions, not merely describe a desirable quality.

## Purpose and context

Identify the experience the interface needs to support from the request and available project evidence. Separate what is known about its audience and use from assumptions. An internal comparison tool, a learning experience, and an expressive product introduction can require different emphasis without one being inherently more polished.

GOV.UK and USWDS both ground design in people's needs and context. Their service missions are specific to government; the transferable lesson is to connect a choice to its purpose, not to copy government presentation or turn every small edit into a research project. Use existing evidence and resolve only uncertainties that materially affect the task. [GOV.UK principles](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/government-design-principles), [USWDS principles](https://designsystem.digital.gov/design-principles/)

## A useful design philosophy

Translate a broad intention into a decision rule with an observable consequence. “Calm” might mean limiting simultaneous emphasis during a demanding task; “expressive” might mean giving imagery and typography a stronger role in discovery. These are contextual interpretations, not fixed definitions.

Explain a relevant tradeoff: what receives emphasis, what stays secondary, and what should remain stable across the experience. A principle that permits every possible treatment does not help. A principle that prescribes every pixel prevents appropriate adaptation.

For an established product, understand the existing rationale before changing it. A repeated choice is evidence of a convention, not proof of intentional quality. Distinguish a local inconsistency from a request to change the product's direction.

## References and inspiration

Describe what a reference contributes: the relationship between content and layout, the density, the character of the imagery, or an interaction idea. Then check whether that quality fits this product's actual content and constraints. Do not make an unexplained product imitation the acceptance criterion.

When references conflict, identify whether they address different moments or express incompatible priorities. A coherent design can combine influences, but each choice needs a role in the resulting system. Do not import unrelated navigation, decorative sections, or invented claims to resemble a reference.

A screenshot is useful visual evidence. It does not establish its author's intent, complete behavior, or success with users.

## Positive and negative guidance

Pair the desired behavior with the failure it avoids and the context where the distinction matters. Use examples as explanations, not universal bans.

- For repeated comparison work, prioritize inspectable content and stable controls. Filling the opening view with decorative summaries may delay the task; a summary that changes the user's next decision can still be useful.
- For a visually expressive experience, make image selection, typography, and composition reinforce the intended character. Adding a gradient or changing a radius without a coherent direction does not explain the improvement.
- For consistency, preserve recognizable relationships and meanings. Requiring every screen to use the same composition despite different information can work against that goal.

A restrained or expressive treatment can both be appropriate. Novelty, minimalism, and familiarity are possible means, not independent proof of quality.

## Choosing and evolving a direction

Compare alternatives against the same content, task, and constraints so the actual design differences are visible. Produce alternatives when they resolve a meaningful choice; do not require multiple mockups for routine work.

State the reason for the recommended direction and the uncertainty that could change it. Use [design review](design-review.md) for a structured comparison and [visual design](visual-design.md) to develop the expression. Record a durable principle in the project's existing design authority when future work depends on it; do not create a separate philosophy document by default.

## Basis and limits

This reference is an authoring synthesis informed by the cited design principles. Its examples illustrate judgment; they are not research results or a claim that one visual style improves task success. Existing user direction and project constraints remain part of the decision.
